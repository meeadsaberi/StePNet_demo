# StePNet demo: pedestrian flows in Sydney and NYC

Interactive maps of estimated pedestrian volumes on every sidewalk link, from the [StePNet](https://github.com/fanmdi/StepNet) model calibrated to local pedestrian counts.

## Method

**1. Transferred demand.** Demand models are estimated in a source city and applied to the target city without re-estimation (Brisbane → Sydney, Seattle → NYC), following Nourmohammadi, Rashidi & Saberi (2026).
- *Trip generation:* a negative binomial count model gives walking trips produced in each zone (SA1 in Sydney), μᵢ = exp(β₀ + Σₘ βₘ xₘᵢ), using socio-demographic and built-environment variables.
- *Destination choice:* a graph neural network trained in the source city scores each origin–destination pair on the walking network; scores are turned into probabilities with a sparse (sparsemax) transformation, so each origin has a limited set of feasible destinations. OD demand is Tₒd = Tₒ · pₒd.

**2. Route assignment.** Each OD pair is assigned to up to 10 distinct routes (pairwise overlap below 50%), generated by repeated shortest paths with link penalties; in Sydney these add to the routes precomputed in the StePNet repository. Each zone is connected to the network at 5 points, with its demand split evenly between them. Route shares follow a C-Logit model using route length, number of turns and a commonality factor for overlapping routes. Link flows sum the flows of all routes that use the link (single pass, no congestion feedback).

**3. Calibration.** OD demand is adjusted to match counts by solving a regularised inverse problem (as in the paper):

min (1/|I|) Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + δ (1/|Z|) Σ_od (q̂_od − p_od)²,  with q̂_od ≥ 0 and δ = 0.001,

where yᵢ are observed counts, ŷᵢ the modelled flows on the counted links, and p_od the prior. Unlike the paper, the prior is first scaled by one citywide factor (total observed ÷ total modelled at count sites), so p_od = k · q⁰_od. OD pairs that pass no count stay at the scaled value rather than the original, lower one. The problem is solved with bounded least squares (trust-region reflective); the iteration limit acts as early stopping and is set where the fit reaches R² ≈ 0.80.

**4. Validation.** Count sites are split at random into 5 groups. Each group is left out in turn, the factor and calibration are re-fitted on the other 80%, and the left-out sites are predicted. Every site is therefore predicted once without its own count. The maps show both the calibration fit (all counts used) and this validation.

## Limitations

- The validation shows how well the maps fill gaps between nearby counts. Accuracy drops noticeably in areas with no counts nearby, so estimates there should be treated as indicative.
- NYC counts cover a single peak hour, while the transferred demand covers a longer period. The citywide factor therefore combines a time-window conversion with a level correction. All three NYC periods start from the same demand matrix, rescaled.
- Calibration is fitted to street flows, not trip totals. Total trips and average trip length (about 1.3 km in NYC and 1.9 km in Sydney) depend on the transferred destination choice and are less certain than the street flows.
- Total walking trip numbers are less certain than street volumes. We estimate roughly 1.4 to 2.5 million walking trips per weekday in the City of Sydney, and roughly 15 to 25 million per weekday across New York City.
